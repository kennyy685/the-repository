## Useful details

### The inspection as a sales moment
- Walking the perimeter together (not alone) turns the inspection into something the homeowner witnesses rather
  than takes on faith - matches CLAUDE.md's "document damage, don't control the claim" line.
- Order matters: point at the damage first, photograph it with the homeowner present, explain it right after -
  doing photography off to the side alone reads as less trustworthy than doing it together.
- A side-by-side comparison photo (clean area vs. damaged area) is the single most homeowner-legible piece of
  evidence cited across sources - easier to grasp than a close-up alone.
- The finding should be stated as one repeatable sentence the homeowner can pass on to a spouse or the adjuster
  later, phrased about the house's condition, never about what the insurer will do.
- The "next step" at the end of an inspection should be a named form (inspection authorization) and a specific
  day - never a vague "I'll be in touch," matching round 27's "one small ask" rule.

### Price objections, cash jobs, good/better/best
- The standard framework across sources: **validate the concern -> ask what specifically feels high (price
  itself, timing, or a competing bid) -> offer a path** (show the middle tier, explain financing, or do a
  side-by-side scope comparison) - never a straight discount.
- Presenting 3 tiers (Good/Better/Best) *before* a price objection lands, with the priciest tier shown first, is
  the anchoring technique sources point to; it's presentation order, not new content, since O7 already plans
  Good/Better/Best for the cash path.
- One search-summary source claims 60-70% of buyers land on the middle tier when tiers are framed this way, and
  that a roughly 13% price step between tiers is typical in the industry - both are single-source claims, flagged
  unverified below, useful as a starting proportion, not a guarantee.
- "I got a cheaper quote" objections are best met by asking to see the competing scope rather than matching the
  price - a lower number very often hides a thinner warranty or downgraded material, which is the actual
  differentiator to point at.
